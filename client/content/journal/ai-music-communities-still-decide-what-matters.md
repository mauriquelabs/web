In a recent open letter, SoundCloud CEO Eliah Seton argued that AI is changing music, and that its future must be built around artists. Not around the tools alone. Around the people who make the work, and the communities that give that work meaning.

We are not SoundCloud. We are product builders working at the intersection of technology, music, and shared experiences. Still, the letter names a tension we keep running into.

AI makes it easier to generate sound, drafts, variations, and finished-looking work. It does not automatically make it easier to know what deserves to exist, who it is for, or how it should live inside a real community.

## The false binary is not useful

Too often, AI in music is framed as all opportunity or all threat. Embrace everything, or reject everything.

That framing is too blunt for the work.

New tools have always changed how music is made: sampling, synthesizers, auto-tune, digital production. Each one expanded creative possibility. Each one also forced culture to renegotiate authorship, craft, and trust.

AI is another shift in that lineage. The question is not whether it arrives. The question is who it serves while it does.

Seton’s point is useful here: innovation and artist protection should evolve together. For product builders, that translates into a practical discipline. Speed without stewardship is not progress. It is just volume.

## Creative communities are the fragile part

Music has never been created only in isolation. Scenes form through shared rooms, shared nights, shared references, and the awkward early experiments people try in front of each other.

That is easy to forget when software can produce endless output alone.

If AI displaces the spaces where artists connect, collaborate, and inspire one another, we do not just change production. We thin out the culture that makes new sounds travel, mutate, and matter.

At Maurique Labs, this is close to home. Through electronic music and projects like Mind the Beat, we care about discovery, belonging, and the experiences that bring people together. Through experiments like AI Sound Labs, we are also curious about what AI can unlock for creative expression.

Those two instincts can reinforce each other. They can also collide.

Being able to generate more music faster is not the same as building a healthier scene.

## Authenticity is a product decision

SoundCloud’s updates against AI slop, spam, and deceptive personas are platform policy. They are also a product lesson.

When creation becomes cheap, trust becomes expensive.

Listeners do not only ask “is this interesting?” They start asking “is this real?”, “who made this?”, and “can I trust what this platform is showing me?” Artists ask whether their work can still be found, credited, and protected.

For product builders, authenticity is not a mood. It is a set of choices:

- What we allow into a system
- What we label and make transparent
- What we refuse to amplify
- Whose rights we design around from the start

We do not need to copy SoundCloud’s policies to learn from the principle. If we build tools or experiences around music and AI, we should decide early what kind of creative community we are willing to protect.

## What this means for how we build

Our working conviction is simple.

Technology gives us the ability to turn ideas into reality. Communities help us understand which ideas matter.

AI strengthens the first half of that sentence. It does not replace the second.

So when we explore AI in music, we want to ask better questions than “can we generate this?”

- Does this help artists connect, learn, or ship work they stand behind?
- Does this make discovery more meaningful, or just noisier?
- Does this respect the people and scenes the work comes from?
- Would this still be worth building if it could not scale overnight?

We do not have all of those answers yet. That is fine. Journaling this early is part of the point.
